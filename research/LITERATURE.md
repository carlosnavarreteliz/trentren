# Earthquake forecasting in Chile: literature and state-of-the-art review

Written 2026-10-17 for the parallel hypothesis-testing effort in `research/`. Scope: what models are used or proposed for Chile, what each claimed, what survived independent testing, and whether Chilean data exist to test it. It ends with a ranked list of under-explored variables.

Confidence labels used below:
- **[V]** I checked this claim against a web source during this review (URL given).
- **[K]** From my background knowledge of the literature. The reference is standard, but I did not re-fetch it today. Check the DOI before citing.

---

## 0. Bottom line

1. **No method has passed prospective, independent testing as a deterministic predictor** (time, place and magnitude within narrow windows) anywhere, Chile included. The operational state of the art is probabilistic: long-term PSHA plus short-term clustering models (ETAS/STEP-like).
2. **Clustering dominates short-term forecast skill.** In CSEP experiments (California, Italy, Japan, New Zealand, global), ETAS-type models consistently beat smoothed-seismicity Poisson models. Physics-based Coulomb/rate-state hybrids and neural point processes sometimes match ETAS or beat it by a few percent of information gain per event. Nothing beats it by a large margin.
3. **The only exogenous or non-catalog input with replicated prospective gain is geodetic strain rate as a long-term spatial prior** (GEAR1: smoothed seismicity combined with GSRM strain rate, Bird et al. 2015; Strader et al. 2018). It is spatial, not temporal, so it does not predict *when*.
4. **The time-dependent precursors best documented for Chile are the 2014 Iquique and 2017 Valparaíso foreshock and slow-slip sequences.** They were identified *retrospectively*. The 2015 Illapel Mw 8.3 had few precursors and the 2010 Maule Mw 8.8 had little, so there is no consistent pattern.
5. **Our test period has very little statistical power.** The USGS catalog in our box holds about 6 M≥7 events from 2013 to 2026 (Iquique 8.2 and 7.7, Illapel 8.3 and 7.0, Chiloé 7.6, the 2024 San Pedro de Atacama 7.4 intraslab at 127 km). Only about 4 of these are independent mainshocks, and there are about 105 M≥6 events (`chile_usgs_1900_2026.csv`, computed today). A large-event predictor cannot reach Bonferroni significance (α=0.0036) on 4 to 5 independent targets unless its effect is enormous. **Primary targets should be M≥5 to 6 with information-gain-per-earthquake (IGPE) metrics against ETAS.** M≥7 should be treated as descriptive only.
6. **Catalog-composition warning (leakage and nonstationarity).** In our USGS file, annual event counts reach 2,000 to 3,500 in 1998–2008 and fall to 600–950 from 2011 on. The number of M≥4.5 events is far steadier (about 150–300 per year outside aftershock years). Contributed small-magnitude networks changed, and the train/test split at 2013 falls near the post-Maule (2010) and post-Iquique (2014) densification of the CSN and IPOC networks. Any feature built from counts below about M4.5, or from `nst/gap/dmin/rms/net/magType`, will carry network-history artifacts.

## 1. Operational and research forecasting models used for Chile

### 1.1 CSN (Centro Sismológico Nacional, U. de Chile) practice
- CSN runs the national network: about 65+ broadband stations, about 300 strong-motion stations and about 130 GNSS stations, most installed after 2012 under the ONEMI/SENAPRED expansion [K]. It publishes locations, magnitudes, moment tensors and early tsunami information (with SHOA). **I found no evidence that CSN issues operational probabilistic aftershock or short-term forecasts** [V: search returned only academic ETAS studies]. Official communication is deterministic: event reports, plus statements that earthquakes cannot be predicted.
- The USGS issues generic aftershock forecasts for significant international events, with parameters from Page et al. (2016, BSSA 106:2290, "Three ingredients for improved global aftershock forecasts") [K]. These provide the de facto operational short-term forecast for Chile.
- Data: CSN catalog at https://www.sismologia.cl and the FDSN service (network C, C1). IPOC (network CX) since 2006: https://www.ipoc-network.org.

### 1.2 ETAS and CSEP-type testing in Chile
- ETAS (Ogata 1988, JASA 83:9) is the standard short-term baseline.
- Chile-specific ETAS work: Nicolis, Chiodi & Adelfio (2017, semi-parametric ETAS with etasFLP, areas around recent M8+ events) and Chiodi et al. (EGU22-7506, anisotropic ETAS for Chile) [V: https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU22/EGU22-7506.html , https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/53217]. These are fits and retrospective forecasts, **not formal CSEP pseudo-prospective tests**.
- Nicolis, Plaza & Salas (2021, *Spatial Statistics*): ETAS-derived daily rate grids passed to an LSTM/CNN to forecast the next-day maximum rate [V via search summary]. The ML model forecasts ETAS output, so its skill cannot exceed the information that ETAS already contains.
- Global and regional ETAS with pseudo-prospective evaluation on subduction zones, which may include Chile: Nandan et al. (2021, global short-term models; 2019 spatially-variable ETAS, which beats spatially-invariant ETAS in California) [V partially: https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1903.07075]. A regionalized subduction ETAS was evaluated pseudo-prospectively for 2015–2018 [V, but the paper's study regions were not confirmed].
- Global CSEP: GEAR1 (Bird et al. 2015, BSSA 105:2538) and Strader et al. (2018, SRL 89:1262) found that merging smoothed seismicity with geodetic strain rate beats either component prospectively. Bayona et al. (2021, 2022, GJI) confirm this [K].
- **Gap: no published Chile-specific CSEP testing center or prospective ETAS benchmark.** Our effort fills an actual gap, and any result should be reported with pyCSEP-style tests (Savran et al. 2022, SRL; https://github.com/SCECcode/pycsep).

### 1.3 UCERF-style and PSHA (GEM, national models)
- UCERF3 (Field et al. 2014, 2017) combines long-term fault-based rates, elastic-rebound time dependence and ETAS. **No UCERF-equivalent exists for Chile.** The pieces exist: segment histories, coupling maps and paleoseismology (Cisternas et al. 2005, Nature 437:404, recurrence of 1960-type events about every 300 years) [K].
- GEM/SARA South America model (v2016 → v2020.1, used in the 2023 global maps) [V: https://hazard.openquake.org/gem/models/SAM , https://www.globalquakemodel.org/product/south-america-hazard]. It is time-independent.
- Poulos et al. (2019, BSSA): an updated recurrence model for Chilean interface and intraslab seismicity, with statistical support for its Poisson nature at the PSHA scale [V: https://repositorio-az.uc.cl/dspace/items/c9190df1-0e02-4a0a-8f73-e4b1e6855dba/full].
- Time-dependent hazard for central-southern Chile (e.g., ITAM records) [V: https://invenio.itam.cas.cz/record/18225].
- The ML ground-motion model for Chilean subduction (2025–26) concerns shaking prediction, not earthquake occurrence [V: https://investigadores.uandes.cl/es/publications/a-machine-learning-based-ground-motion-model-for-the-chilean-subd/].

### 1.4 ML and deep learning (2018–2026)
- **Chile-specific:** Reyes, Morales-Esteban & Martínez-Álvarez (2013, Applied Soft Computing 13:1314), "Neural networks to predict earthquakes in Chile" [K]. It used b-value and Gutenberg-Richter features for 5-day M≥ thresholds in Santiago and Valparaíso and reported high specificity. Its evaluation windows were short, class balance was not addressed and there was no ETAS baseline. Also: Plaza, Salas & Nicolis (2019 preprint, LSTM 2012–2018), a 2024 LMSCAN+LSTM-IC real-time paper (Computers & Electrical Engineering) and a UNAB thesis [V: https://repositorio.unab.cl/xmlui/handle/ria/24403]. **None reports a prospective comparison against ETAS.**
- **Critiques that apply to all of these:** Mignan & Broccardo (2020, SRL 91:2330), a meta-analysis of 77 neural-network earthquake-prediction papers (1994–2019), found pervasive overfitting, leakage and no gain over simple baselines. Mignan & Broccardo (2019, Nature 574:E1) showed that a one-neuron logistic model matches DeVries et al.'s (2018) deep aftershock-location net [K].
- **Credible ML forecasting (non-Chile):** Dascher-Cousineau et al. (2023, GRL, RECAST neural temporal point process; comparable to ETAS, with gains on large catalogs); Stockman, Lawson & Werner (2023, arXiv 2301.09948, neural TPP slightly better than ETAS on the Amatrice-Norcia sequence); Zlydenko et al. (2023, Sci. Rep., "A neural encoder for earthquake rate forecasting", Japan, about 4% information gain over ETAS and about 1000x faster) [V: https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/a-neural-encoder-for-earthquake-rate-forecasting/]. **These are the honest bar: a few percent gain over ETAS.**
- Lab ML: Rouet-Leduc et al. (2017), Hulbert et al. (2019). Acoustic emission predicts lab failure, and in Cascadia, tremor-band statistics track GNSS slow slip. Neither has been shown to predict large natural earthquakes [K].

### 1.5 AI foundation-style seismic models
- Waveform foundation models: SeisLM (NeurIPS 2024 workshop, contrastive pretraining), SeisMoLLM (arXiv 2502.19960) and SeismoGPT (SciPost preprint 2025) [V: https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/105912 , https://papers.cool/arxiv/2502.19960]. These improve detection, picking and magnitude estimation, so they yield **better catalogs**, not forecasts.
- Catalog foundation models: QuakeGPT (SCEC 2025 poster) is trained on *simulated* catalogs and is still a proposal [V: https://central.scec.org/node/59093].
- Indirect value for Chile: ML-enhanced catalogs (PhaseNet/EQTransformer on IPOC data, e.g., Sippl et al. 2023 for northern Chile with about 1.5 million events [K]) lower Mc by about 1 to 2 units. That enables b-positive, repeater and foreshock analyses that the USGS catalog cannot support.

## 3. What survived (short list)
1. **Clustering (ETAS)** gives most of the short-term skill. Any new variable must add information *conditional on* ETAS.
2. **Spatial priors**: smoothed seismicity, geodetic strain rate and coupling. These have prospective CSEP support (GEAR1).
3. **Foreshock and slow-slip sequences** are real phenomena in Chile (Iquique 2014, Valparaíso 2017), but they are not unique to mainshocks. Most swarms and SSEs are not followed by a large event, so the false-alarm rate is high.
4. **Small modulations** (tides, hydrological loading, dynamic triggering) are real in some settings at the few-percent rate level. They are statistically detectable with large catalogs and practically useless for prediction.
5. Space weather, LOD, TEC and gravity precursors are contested or unreplicated.

## 5. Methodological notes for the parallel agents (from the literature)
- **Metrics:** use log-likelihood information gain per earthquake (IGPE) relative to ETAS, CSEP N, S, M and T tests (pyCSEP), and Molchan error diagrams for alarm-based claims (Zechar & Jordan 2008). Do not use accuracy or AUC on imbalanced daily bins without an ETAS reference.
- **Declustering:** Gardner-Knopoff, Reasenberg and Zaliapin nearest-neighbor methods give different mainshock sets. Report results for at least two, because precursor claims are often sensitive to this choice (Felzer-style critiques).
- **Surrogates:** circular time-shifts of the exogenous series, ETAS-simulated synthetic catalogs (the strongest null for "precursory activity") and yearly block bootstrap.
- **Effect-size realism:** published credible gains over ETAS are about 2 to 10% IGPE (neural TPPs, CRS hybrids). A claimed 10x gain is almost certainly leakage.
- **Known leakage traps in Chile:** network densification after 2010/2014; USGS `net` and `magType` mixture changes over time; reviewed versus automatic solutions; finite-fault models and coupling maps that use post-event GPS; GNSS stations installed *because of* Iquique or Maule.
